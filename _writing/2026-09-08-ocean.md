It seemed unlikely to me that I was where I was. I fasted. I drove. I stared. 
I had a vague goal in mind---I at least wanted to fart around the Cape Ann area
for a day or two, but beyond that I had no plan, really. I tried to leave 
all my friends' things that were in my car before I left---I left all the CDs and
a well-read copy of *The Gay Science*---but it was haphazard. I'd accidentally
brought First Day Back's album *Forward*, a small marshmallow plush on my dashboard
drove with me, my father's tools in the trunk, etc. 

Turmoil raged in me. I was leaving, but not really. 
I turned my phone off and made it impossible for anyone to find me, but I wrote
postcards back home. I drove local roads so EZPass wouldn't pick me up, but I 
bought some knicknacks to bring back home. *Was* I coming home?

Congress St → High St → Commercial St → Franklin St was the loop I walked for
two hours in Portland, ME. My head was thick enough from starving and sleepy
enough from driving up from Cape Ann that I didn't trust myself to leg it any 
further north. I found a spot that didn't disallow overnight parking and set out.
It was Saturday night, I think. Old Port swarmed with people around my age, all
in groups. I wrote another set of postcards that I bought from a gift shop 
that was open weirdly late. People streamed past me like long exposure. 
I felt sick but not enough to throw up and miserable but not enough to cry. I 
was so alone but not enough to come back home.

I could little more than compare my solitude and the companionship everyone around me seemed to enjoy.
No one else at Salisbury beach was there alone. Everyone I saw leave a restaurant
in Gloucester was with family or friends. I climbed the lighthouse at Pemaquid 
with a couple who came from a part of New York not too far from where I grew up.
Even at the time I realized what a horribly self-obsessed train of thought this
was---obviously, I was just looking for things to make myself miserable. My eyes
doubtlessly glazed over the dozens of lonely people filtering in and out of the
bars in Portland, or the old men enjoying a solitary walk on the beach at Old Orchard,
or the countless runners I'm sure I saw in Rockport. I saw what I wanted to see.

My memories of my trip are scattered and hard to link together. 
[Yang et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2025.108034) suggests that
fasting impairs episodic context memory, perhaps due to suppression of hippocampal 
activity. I remember how I felt, mostly. What I did is still a mystery to me.

I turned my phone off and drove using highway maps from rest stops. I remember 
wanting to blow off work and school and everyone I'd ever known and just keep 
going, to withdraw every cent in my bank accounts and leave my car with a neat little
note, explaining that while I was very terribly sorry for all the fuss that I just
couldn't do it anymore, be in the tangle of your guys's lives, I just couldn't 
look in your eyes again, that I just couldn't brush up against you again, that I
had to run away from this forever, and just keep walking until I disappeared
forever. 

I remember wanting to walk out into the ocean.
